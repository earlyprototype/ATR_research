## 1. The answers in brief

**The Continuous Thought Machine is a neural network that runs its own internal clock, and reads its answer out of which of its neurons agree with each other over time rather than out of what any neuron currently says.** It takes a fixed input, an image or a sequence, and then thinks about it for a set number of steps that the authors call internal ticks, a number chosen by the designer and unrelated to the size or shape of the input. At every tick each neuron looks back over the last 25 values that arrived at it and applies its own private little network to that history, and the model's output is computed from a matrix of pairwise agreements between neurons. This is established from the paper and from the authors' code. Section 2 has the mechanism.

**It meets Potter's work at the level of the observable, not the lineage: the Continuous Thought Machine has made the electrophysiologist's measurement into the model's representation.** Potter's field measures living networks by when their cells fire together; this model computes exactly that class of statistic and then, unusually, uses it as the thing the answer is read from rather than as a summary an experimenter inspects afterwards. That correspondence is real and, I think, the interesting part. It is also unacknowledged: I searched the full 44 pages of the paper for Potter, multi-electrode, dissociated, hybrot, closed-loop, embodiment and in vitro, and there are no matches for any of them. This is established. Section 4 has the three points of contact.

**It misses the part of Potter's programme he cared most about, with one exception nobody has emphasised.** Potter's argument is that a network must be embodied, given a body and a world whose responses depend on what the network did, before you can understand it or teach it anything, and his sharpest result is that the same stimuli replayed without that contingency taught the culture nothing. In the Continuous Thought Machine's headline tasks, image classification, mazes, parity and sorting, the input never changes in response to what the model does, which is precisely Potter's inert control condition. The exception is the paper's reinforcement-learning tasks, which are genuinely closed-loop in Potter's sense and are the least emphasised experiments in the work. That reading is mine, marked as inferred. Section 5 has it.

**For this project it is a new position on the map rather than a competitor, and it is not a counterexample to the claim issue #26 asks us to defend.** That issue claims this project's niche, repeated application of a whole frozen model with re-injection at the level of the internal tensor, is close to empty, and lists five candidate occupants to check. The Continuous Thought Machine is not a sixth: its recurrence is trained into it from scratch, and the thing it repeats is a small recurrent cell, not a transformer stack. Section 6 has what it does change.

## 2. What the Continuous Thought Machine is

The model was published by Sakana AI, a Tokyo research company, as "Continuous Thought Machines" by Luke Darlow, Ciaran Regan, Sebastian Risi, Jeffrey Seely and Llion Jones, submitted to the arXiv preprint server on 8 May 2025 and last revised on 3 October 2025. This is established from the arXiv record. The authors state their own aim plainly: "The goal of this work is to share the CTM and its associated innovations, rather than pushing for new state-of-the-art results." That is a quotation from the abstract, and it is worth keeping in view, because it sets what the numbers in section 3 are for.

Three design decisions define it. All three are established, read from the paper and confirmed against the model source at `models/ctm.py`.

**First, an internal clock that the data does not set.** An ordinary image classifier does its work in one pass: the picture goes in, the layers run once, the answer comes out. This model instead has a loop that runs a fixed number of times, and the authors call each pass an internal tick. The number of ticks is a setting, not a property of the input. In the authors' own code the default is 75 ticks for image classification, for mazes and for the parity task alike, and the ImageNet experiment reported in the paper used 50. The same picture, sitting still, is thought about fifty times over. The paper's phrasing for this is that the model "operates along a self-generated timeline of internal 'thought steps'".

**Second, every neuron has its own small private network, and it processes a history.** In a standard network a neuron receives a single number and applies one fixed function to it. Here each neuron keeps the last 25 numbers that arrived at it, a window the authors call its trace, and applies to that whole window a two-layer network whose weights belong to that neuron and no other. The authors call these neuron-level models. The window length of 25 is the default in all three task configurations in their code. The effect is that a neuron's output depends not on what it is receiving now but on the shape of what it has been receiving, which is why the authors describe the model as having temporal dynamics at the level of the individual neuron.

**Third, and this is the unusual one, the answer is read out of agreement between neurons, not out of the neurons.** At each tick the model takes a chosen set of neurons and computes, for every pair of them, a running product of their activity, discounted so that recent ticks count for more than old ones, with the discount rate itself learned. Stack those pairwise numbers into a vector and that vector, and only that vector, is what the model's output layer sees. The authors call it the synchronisation representation. Two of these are computed: one steers where the model looks in the input, and one produces the prediction.

The sizes are worth stating because they are surprising. For a set of `n` chosen neurons the representation has `n` times `n` plus one, all divided by two, entries, which is every pair plus every neuron with itself. In the ImageNet configuration the authors choose 512 neurons for the output, so the vector the classifier reads is 131,328 numbers wide, built out of 512 neurons. In the maze and parity configurations they choose 32 neurons, giving 528 numbers. The formula is established from the code's own documentation; the two arithmetic results are mine from that formula and the code's default settings.

This project's founding analogy does not transfer here, and it is worth saying so rather than stretching it. The analogy is a tone fed into a room, the echo recorded and fed back in, until the room settles on the note it prefers. The Continuous Thought Machine does not do that. It does not feed its output back in as its input. It holds a fixed input steady, keeps a private internal state, and lets that state evolve on its own clock while attending to the unchanging input. The room is not being re-excited by its own echo; a steady tone is being held while something inside the room rings on a schedule of its own. The two are different mechanisms, and reading one as the other would be a mistake.

One further mechanism matters for section 4, and I read it in the source rather than the prose, at `utils/losses.py`. The model produces a prediction at every single tick, along with a measure of how certain it is, defined as one minus the normalised entropy of the prediction, a quantity that is 1 when the model puts all its weight on one answer and 0 when it spreads it evenly. The training loss is then the average of two things: the loss at whichever tick happened to be most accurate, and the loss at whichever tick the model was most certain. This is established from the code. It means the model is never trained to be right at the end. It is trained to be right somewhere, and to know when. That is the whole of the adaptive-compute story, and it is three lines of code.

## 3. What it has been shown to do

The paper evaluates on ImageNet-1K image classification, 2D maze solving, sorting, parity computation, question answering over MNIST digits, and three reinforcement-learning environments. The table gives the numbers I verified in the paper text, with the scale each one needs to be read against. Everything in it is established from the paper except the final column's baselines, which are marked in the notes.

| Task | What the model must do | Result reported | What to read it against |
|---|---|---|---|
| ImageNet-1K classification | Name which of 1,000 object categories a photograph shows | 72.47 percent top-1 and 89.89 percent top-5 accuracy, using 50 internal ticks and a ResNet-152 image front end | Guessing at random on a 1,000-category problem gives 0.1 percent, one in a thousand. The authors say in the same sentence that they "are not yet aiming for state-of-the-art accuracy", and a conventionally trained ResNet-152, the same front end, is usually reported around the high seventies, which is background knowledge I did not verify this session |
| 2D mazes | Output the route through a maze presented as an image, with no map of the maze given | Trained on mazes of 39 by 39 cells, and the paper reports it "can generalise to longer paths and bigger mazes", tested on 99 by 99 | The comparison model, a long short-term memory network, "los[t] capability after approximately 20 steps" along the path. The paper says the model's own performance "begins to taper off" on the larger mazes, so this is a claim of partial generalisation, not of solving them |
| Cumulative parity | Given a sequence of 64 values each either minus one or plus one, say at every position whether the number of minus ones so far is odd or even | The paper reports that accuracy increases with thinking time, and that models given different tick budgets learn visibly different strategies | Guessing gives 50 percent at each position. The interesting result here is not the accuracy but the strategy, described below |
| Reinforcement learning | Act in an environment over time: CartPole, Acrobot, and a four-room gridworld | Reported as working; the paper does not foreground the numbers | These are the only tasks in the paper where the model's own actions change what it sees next, which is the point section 5 turns on |

Two findings from that set are worth more than the accuracy figures, and both are established from the paper.

The first is the parity strategies. The authors compared a model given 100 internal ticks with one given 75, on the same task, and found they had learned different algorithms. The one with 100 ticks moved its attention "from the beginning to the end of the sequence", raising its certainty position by position as it went. The one with 75 ticks instead "learn[ed] to attend to the sequence in reverse order, accurately predicting the parity of the majority of the sequence simultaneously during the final internal ticks", which the authors read as "a form of planning, building up its understanding of the observed data before making a final decision". The number of ticks available changed the algorithm discovered, which is a result about the clock and not about the task.

The second is calibration, which means the correspondence between how confident a model says it is and how often it is actually right, a property that most image classifiers are notoriously bad at without special treatment. The authors report "excellent model calibration" on ImageNet, and on the smaller CIFAR-10 dataset report that the model "demonstrates the best calibration, even when compared to humans", the human comparison coming from a published dataset of human answers on the same images. Given the loss described at the end of section 2, which explicitly rewards the model for being certain at the tick where it is correct, good calibration is close to being what the model was directly trained for. That last sentence is an inference of mine, not a claim the paper makes.

## 4. Where it meets Potter's work

The intersection is real, and it is at the level of the measured quantity. Three points of contact, in descending order of how firmly I hold them.

**The model's representation is an electrophysiologist's statistic.** Potter's laboratory studied living cortical networks grown on a multi-electrode array, which is a small chip carrying a grid of electrodes that can both listen to and stimulate the cells sitting on it. What such an instrument gives you is timing: which cells fired, and when, relative to each other. The summaries that field works in are of that kind, and this project's literature register already records one of them by name, the Center of Activity Trajectory of Chao, Bakkum and Potter (2007), which tracks where the activity-weighted centre of firing moves across the array over time. The Continuous Thought Machine computes a quantity of the same class, a discounted running agreement between every pair of a chosen set of neurons, and then does something no electrophysiologist can do: it makes that quantity the representation the system itself acts on, rather than a summary the experimenter reads afterwards. That the model does this is established from the code and the paper. That it amounts to promoting the observable into the mechanism is my reading, marked as inferred.

**Both positions agree, from opposite directions, that undifferentiated global synchrony carries nothing.** Potter's clearest dynamical result, Wagenaar, Madhavan, Pine and Potter (2005), concerns the globally synchronised burst, the mode in which nearly the whole culture falls silent and then fires together, over and over. The paper's hypothesis was that this persists because a dish receives none of the input a real cortex gets from elsewhere in the brain, and their intervention, rapid distributed closed-loop stimulation, dispersed it. In that programme global synchrony is the pathology to be treated. In the Continuous Thought Machine synchronisation is the desirable representation, which reads at first like a flat contradiction. It is not one. If all 512 of the model's output neurons moved together, every one of the 131,328 pairwise agreements would carry nearly the same number, and the representation the classifier reads would collapse to almost a single value and could not distinguish 1,000 categories. What the model needs is the same thing Potter's cultures lacked, which is differentiated timing relations rather than everybody at once. So the two agree on the substance and differ only on which word carries it. This is an inference from the architecture; the paper makes no such argument, and I could find no passage where it addresses the case.

**The measurement-convention hazard is the same on both sides.** Potter's 2008 meeting paper "How should we think about bursts?" argues that measurement conventions manufacture the phenomenon, and this project's issue #26 already records that as the same error class as this project's own aliasing failure, in which a sampling schedule that looked only at even iterations could not, in principle, see a two-state cycle. The Continuous Thought Machine's internal tick is exactly such a convention: it is a chosen grid, set by the designer, and everything observed about the model's dynamics is observed on it. The authors report seeing "low-frequency traveling waves" moving across their neurons, citing the cortical travelling-wave literature of Muller and colleagues (2018). To their credit they immediately withhold interpretation: "We do not assign functional meaning to these observed waves but highlight their distinct presence during the CTM's thought process." That quotation is established. The observation that the wave frequency and the tick grid cannot be disentangled without varying the grid is mine, and is speculation rather than a defect I have demonstrated, since the parity result in section 3 shows they did vary tick counts elsewhere.

One negative finding, cleanly established, belongs here. The paper does not cite Potter, and does not cite the closed-loop or cultured-network literature at all. I searched the extracted full text of all 44 pages for potter, multi-electrode and multielectrode, dissociated, hybrot, closed-loop, embodied and embodiment, and in vitro, and every one of those searches returned nothing. Its neuroscience citations are to Uhlhaas, Pipa, Lima, Melloni, Neuenschwander, Nikolic and Singer (2009) on neural synchrony in cortical networks, and to Muller, Chavane, Reynolds and Sejnowski (2018) on cortical travelling waves. The authors are explicit about the level at which they are borrowing: "While inspired by principles like spike-timing and synchrony, CTM abstracts these, focusing on local temporal integration and population-level synchronization, into a tractable, differentiable framework suitable for gradient-based deep learning, rather than replicating detailed biophysics." So the intersection with Potter is a convergence of ideas and not a debt, and should be described that way in anything we write.

## 5. Where it does not meet Potter's work, and the one place it does

Three disanalogies, and then the exception.

**Nothing learns during the loop.** Potter's programme is about a loop that changes the network. The learning runs on activity-dependent plasticity, meaning real synapses whose strengths move as a consequence of the activity passing through them, and his sharpest result, Bakkum, Chao and Potter (2008), is that a sequence of training stimuli that had worked produced neither the plasticity nor the behaviour when replayed without being contingent on the network's ongoing performance. The contingency, not the stimuli, was the mechanism. The Continuous Thought Machine's weights are fixed once training ends; its ticks traverse a landscape that gradient descent laid down in advance. Its internal clock is a readout clock, not a learning clock. This is established, and it is the same disanalogy the committed Potter study already records against this project's own loop in its section 6.2.

**There is no body and no world.** In the classification, maze, parity, sorting and question-answering tasks, the model attends to an input that sits still and never answers back. Whatever the model concludes at tick 30 does not alter what it sees at tick 31. In Potter's vocabulary that is an open loop, and it is precisely the condition his 2008 experiment showed to be inert. This is established from the paper and the code, in which the input features are computed once, before the tick loop begins, and never recomputed.

**The neurons are not spiking, and synchronisation here is not coincident firing.** Potter's synchrony is a fact about the timing of discrete electrical events in living cells. The model's synchronisation is a discounted product of two continuous-valued activations. The authors say as much themselves in the passage quoted at the end of section 4. The word is shared; the referent is not. This is established.

Now the exception, and it is the observation I would most want tested by someone who knows the reinforcement-learning literature better than I do. Three of the paper's tasks are reinforcement-learning environments: CartPole and Acrobot, both control problems in which a physical simulation responds to the actions taken, and MiniGrid FourRooms, a gridworld the agent moves through. I verified these from the task directory of the authors' code. In those three, and only in those three, the model acts, the environment changes because of the action, and the changed environment returns as the next input. That is a closed sensory-motor loop in exactly the sense Potter's 2001 animat and his later hybrots were built to create, the difference being that his loop ran through living tissue and this one does not. So the Continuous Thought Machine already contains a small embodied experiment, and it is the part of the paper that gets the least attention: the authors report the reinforcement-learning work without foregrounding numbers for it. If anyone wanted to put Potter's question to this architecture, that is where the apparatus already exists. This reading is inferred, and the judgement that it is under-emphasised is mine.

## 6. What this changes for this project

Four consequences, none of which requires running anything.

**It is not a counterexample to the empty-niche claim, and that is a concrete answer to an open task.** Issue #26 in the companion repository asks that the claim "dynamical-systems interpretability of transformers is nascent, and ATR's niche of whole-map tensor-level re-injection is close to empty" be re-verified against five listed candidate occupants before it is written down anywhere. The Continuous Thought Machine is the obvious sixth candidate to raise, since it is a recurrent dynamical model with an explicit internal time axis. It does not occupy the niche, for two reasons that are established from its code. Its recurrence is trained into it from scratch, so it says nothing about what a frozen pretrained model does under iteration; and the thing it repeats is a small recurrent cell of its own design, not a whole pretrained transformer stack. The niche claim survives this particular challenge. Recording that in issue #26 is worth more than the paper itself is to us.

**It is the far end of an axis this project has already drawn.** The Coconut record in `_STAGE2_JSPACE/FUTURE_WORK.md` sets up a comparison between recurrence that is trained in and recurrence that is imposed on a frozen model, with Coconut fine-tuning a pretrained GPT-2 to feed its own hidden state back in, and this project's loop doing something similar with no training at all. The Continuous Thought Machine belongs on that same axis, past Coconut: not a pretrained model taught to use recurrence, but a model that has nothing else. If that axis is ever drawn in a figure, it now has three points on it rather than two. This is inferred, and it costs nothing to adopt.

**It is an existence proof that the readout space need not be the state space.** This project's current line of work asks whether the states the loop settles into lie in directions the model could put into words, which is the question EXP_011 and EXP_017 are built around. The Continuous Thought Machine is an architecture in which that separation is deliberate and total: the output layer never sees a neuron's value, only the agreements between neurons, so the space the answer is read from is by construction a different space from the one the state lives in. Whether that is useful to us as more than a framing device I do not know, and saying otherwise would be speculation.

**It connects to a group already on our reading list.** The paper cites Jacobs, Budzinski, Muller, Ba and Keller (2025), "Traveling waves integrate spatial information into spectral representations", arXiv 2502.06034. Issue #26 already lists Jacobs, Fel, Hakim, Brondetta, Ba and Keller (2025), "Block-Recurrent Dynamics in Vision Transformers", arXiv 2512.19941, and describes it as methodologically very close to this project's convergence gate. Three authors are common to both. This project and the Continuous Thought Machine are, without either knowing it, reading the same laboratory. This is established from the two reference lists.

## 7. What remains, and what needs the operator's decision

What happened: this note read the Continuous Thought Machine paper in full from the publisher's PDF and its authors' code at a pinned commit, set the result against the committed Potter study and this project's literature register, and found three genuine points of contact, three disanalogies, and one exception that nobody appears to have named. The new facts, none of which existed in this repository before today, are these: the paper contains no reference to Potter or to any closed-loop or cultured-network work, verified by exhaustive search of the extracted text; the model's reinforcement-learning tasks are the only closed-loop experiments in it, and are its least emphasised; the model's adaptive compute is produced by a three-line training loss that averages the most accurate tick with the most certain one; and the paper cites a 2025 paper from the same laboratory as a work already on this project's reading list.

What it means: the intersection the question asked about is real but is a convergence rather than an inheritance, and it sits at the level of the measured quantity, timing relations between units, which one tradition measures from the outside and this architecture computes on the inside. That reading is inferred. The more useful consequence for us is negative and firm: the model does not occupy the niche that issue #26 asks us to defend, and it should be recorded there as a checked-and-cleared candidate rather than as a threat.

What remains, in order of information gained per unit cost, none of it started:

1. Record the paper in issue #26 with the positioning above, so the next person to touch the empty-niche claim does not have to re-derive it. Cost: minutes, and it is the only item here I would do without further instruction.
2. Decide the standing question issue #26 has carried since 2026-07-25, which is whether the biological-analogue framing belongs in the repository or stays as background reading. The Continuous Thought Machine is new evidence for that decision, because it shows a well-received machine-learning paper reaching for the same neuroscience without the closed-loop literature. Cost: a reading and a ruling, no compute.
3. Read the paper's Appendix H, on the recursive computation of the synchronisation matrix, against this project's own convergence gate. The two are both discounted running statistics over an iterated state, and if the mathematics coincides that is a citable technical link rather than an atmospheric one. Cost: an hour of reading, no compute.
4. If the embodiment question is ever to be put to a machine, the paper's reinforcement-learning code is the cheapest existing apparatus for it, since it already closes a sensory-motor loop and its environments are standard. Cost: unknown, and I would not charter it without a hypothesis worth the trouble.
